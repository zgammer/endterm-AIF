Chapter VILearning by Wandering

Now the map is gone. The knight does not know the odds of a slip or the price of a step, and nobody tells him the right move. He can only act, watch what happens, and remember. This is reinforcement learning: state St → action At → reward Rt+1 → next state St+1 → update what he knows. Each step leaves a little record (s, a, r, s′), and a chain of them is a trajectory. Unlike a student with an answer key, he learns from consequences, and the reward for a good deed may come many steps late.

Chapter VIBrave or Careful?

Exploit means choosing the action you currently believe is best. Explore means trying something uncertain to find out whether your beliefs are wrong. Too little exploring traps the knight in a mediocre habit; too much wastes his gold. The simplest compromise is ε-greedy: with probability ε pick a random action, otherwise pick the best known one. With ε = 0.1 about one move in ten is a gamble. Often ε shrinks as training goes on.

Three knobs, three different jobs:

KnobQuestion it answers
α, learning rateHow far should one experience move my belief?
γ, discountHow much do future rewards matter?
ε, explorationHow often do I try something else?

Chapter VISurprise Is a Teacher

Monte Carlo waits for the whole journey, computes the real return G, and nudges the estimate toward it: V(s) ← V(s) + α[G − V(s)]. Temporal-Difference (TD) learning does not wait. After one step it compares its guess with a reward plus another guess:

δ = Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St)

Positive δ means the outcome was better than expected, so the estimate rises; negative δ lowers it. Example: V(A) = 5, reward 2, V(B) = 8, γ = 0.9. The target is 2 + 0.9·8 = 9.2 and the error is 9.2 − 5 = 4.2. A was better than predicted.

Using one guess to improve another is called bootstrapping.

Chapter VIThe Q-Learning Ledger

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

Read it as: current belief plus a fraction α of the surprise. Suppose Q(A,RIGHT) = 4, r = 2, max Q(B,·) = 8, γ = 0.9, α = 0.5. Target = 2 + 0.9·8 = 9.2; error = 5.2; new Q = 4 + 0.5·5.2 = 6.6. Experience beat the expectation, so RIGHT became more valuable.

target   = reward + gamma * best_future_q
td_error = target - current_q
new_q    = current_q + alpha * td_error

Chapter VIPractice on a Chain

Try it on a five-state chain: START, 1, 2, 3, GOAL. The actions are LEFT and RIGHT, each step costs −1, the goal pays +10, and the Q-table starts as all zeros: one row per state, one column per action. In early episodes the knight wanders (0→1→0→1→2→1→2→3→4). After many episodes every state learns that RIGHT is best (0→1→2→3→4), and the policy π(s) = argmaxa Q(s,a) can be read straight from the table.

Chapter VITwo Temperaments

Q-learning and SARSA differ by one small term. Q-learning's target uses max Q(s′,a′): it learns the value of acting greedily afterwards, even while the knight explores. That makes it off-policy. SARSA uses Q(s′,a′) for the action a′ he actually took, so it learns the value of the exploring behavior itself. That makes it on-policy, and it tends to be more cautious when mistakes are costly.

Another divide: model-based methods learn or use a model of transitions and rewards, then plan with it. Model-free methods like Q-learning learn values or policies directly from experience. Neither needs the map handed to it in advance.

Chapter VIWhen the Ledger Overflows

A table works for a chain of five squares. A battery controller with 10 charge levels, 20 prices, 20 demands, 20 solar levels and 24 hours has 1,920,000 states; with 3 actions that is 5,760,000 entries. Images and continuous sensors are worse. So we replace the table with a function that generalizes.

Deep Q-Network (DQN) approximates Q(s,a;θ) with a neural network and stays stable thanks to a replay buffer (random old experiences) and a target network (a slowly updated copy). Policy-gradient methods learn the policy πθ(a|s) directly, which suits continuous actions. Actor–Critic pairs an actor (“what should I do?”) with a critic (“how good was that?”).

The idea is the same as before: long-term action value. Only the way we store it has changed.

Chapter VIFrom the Garden to the Real World

In a factory, a state may be (temperature, vibration, load, health), actions are CONTINUE, REDUCE LOAD, INSPECT or MAINTAIN, and the reward balances output against energy, upkeep and the cost of failure. Careful practice matters:

  • A big reward is not permission to break engineering limits, so hard safety limits should be kept apart from the reward.
  • Train in a simulator or on recorded data before touching real machines.
  • Watch for sensor faults, changing conditions and a badly written reward that pays for the number but not the real goal.
  • Check whether RL is needed at all. Simple optimization may be enough. Compare against strong baselines.

A good RL project starts with a good decision problem, not with a favorite algorithm.

Chapter VIThe Trial of Learning

1. Why does Q-learning use max Q(s′,a′) in its target?

Look at what the max picks: the best next action.
The max makes the target follow the greedy policy, so Q-learning is off-policy: it learns the best behavior even while exploring.

2. A TD error δ is positive. What does that mean?

δ is target minus current estimate.
A positive δ means the target exceeded the current guess, so the guess is raised.

3. Which knob sets how often the agent tries a random action?

One knob is about exploring, one about update size, one about the future.
ε is the chance of an exploratory move; α sets update size and γ weights the future.

4. How does SARSA differ from Q-learning?

The name lists the tuple it uses: (s, a, r, s′, a′).
SARSA's target uses Q(s′,a′) for the action the behavior policy actually chose.

5. Why does a plain Q-table stop working for large problems?

Think about how many cells the battery example needs.
A table needs one entry per state-action pair, which becomes impossible for millions of states, images or continuous sensors.
