Chapter VIIWeighing Fate

Our knight has learned to search, to guess and to learn from wandering. Now he must choose when the world will not tell him what comes next. A rational agent looks at the evidence it has right now, weighs the possible outcomes and picks the best action. It is judged by that choice, not by hindsight. If the dice fall badly, the choice was not necessarily foolish. An omniscient agent, one who already knows the future, does not exist in real life, so every real agent chooses under doubt.

Chapter VIIThe Rule of the Best Bet

The agent repeats a loop: sensors observe, a belief state guesses what is happening, a decision rule compares the actions, and actuators carry one out. The decision rule is this:

a* = arg maxa E[ U | a, e ]

Here a is a candidate action, e is the evidence at hand, U is the utility (a score of how much we like an outcome) and E is the probability-weighted average. Note that arg max returns the action, not the best score.

A delivery robot may take a short route or a safe route. The shortest path is not always the best decision once collision risk and delay costs are counted.

Chapter VIIOdds Are Not Enough

A 90% chance of success sounds fine, until failure is a catastrophe. So a rational agent combines probability and utility. The expected utility of an action is the sum, over its outcomes, of probability times utility:

EU(a) = Σi P(oi | a) × U(oi)

A90% → +10010% → −200.9·100 + 0.1·(−20) = 88

Project B is riskier, yet it wins by maximum expected utility (MEU), because its rewards are larger.

Chapter VIIBrave, Careful or Neutral?

People differ in how they treat risk. A risk-neutral chooser maximizes the plain average and has utility that grows in a straight line with money. A risk-averse chooser dislikes large losses, so their utility curve is concave and they may prefer the steadier prize. If safety rules forbid some actions, we maximize only over the feasible ones.

actions = {'A': ([0.9, 0.1], [100, -20]),
           'B': ([0.6, 0.4], [200, -50])}
def EU(probs, utils):
    return sum(p*u for p, u in zip(probs, utils))
scores = {n: EU(*v) for n, v in actions.items()}
print(max(scores, key=scores.get))   # B

Four lines, and the scroll names the winner: multiply, add, take the arg max.

Chapter VIIUpdating What We Believe

The true state may be hidden, and sensors are noisy. Bayes' theorem updates a belief when evidence D arrives about a hypothesis H (say, a failing machine):

P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D)

The denominator counts both ways the evidence can appear: P(D) = P(D|H)P(H) + P(D|¬H)P(¬H). Forget the false alarms and the posterior is badly inflated.

Try it. A mill machine fails 5% of the time. An alarm sounds for 90% of real failures and also for 10% of healthy runs. Then P(alarm) = 0.9·0.05 + 0.1·0.95 = 0.14, and P(failure | alarm) = 0.045 / 0.14 ≈ 0.32. A loud alarm lifts the risk from 5% to about 32%, not to 90%.

Chapter VIIFrom Belief to Action

Bayes tells us what to believe; decision theory tells us what to do. If the failure probability is p and a failure costs Cfailure, then continuing has expected cost p × Cfailure. Maintenance costs Cmaint and is assumed to prevent the failure. So:

Maintain if pfailure > Cmaint / Cfailure

With maintenance at $2,000 and failure at $10,000, the threshold is 2,000 / 10,000 = 0.20:

Our mill's alarm gave about 32%, so the knight orders maintenance. The 20% comes from costs, not from model accuracy: if failure were costlier, the threshold would drop.

Chapter VIIThe Trial of Reason

1. A rational agent's choice turns out badly. What does that show?

Ask whether the decision used the information it had.
Rationality is about maximizing expected performance given available evidence, so a bad result does not prove a bad choice.

2. Project B: 60% chance of +200, 40% chance of −50. What is its expected utility?

Compute 0.6 × 200 and 0.4 × (−50), then add.
120 − 20 = 100, higher than Project A's 88.

3. Maintenance costs $2,000 and an unprevented failure costs $10,000. At which failure risk does maintenance become worthwhile?

Divide the maintenance cost by the failure cost.
The threshold is 2,000 / 10,000 = 0.20.

4. Route A: 80% for +100, 20% for −50. Route B: 95% for +80, 5% for −20. Which is better by MEU?

Compute both expected utilities.
EU(A) = 80 − 10 = 70 and EU(B) = 76 − 1 = 75, so Route B wins despite its smaller reward.

5. Failure risk is 15%, a failure costs $20,000 and maintenance costs $2,000. What should we do?

The threshold here is 2,000 / 20,000, not 20%.
0.15 × 20,000 = 3,000 > 2,000, so maintenance saves $1,000 in expectation.
